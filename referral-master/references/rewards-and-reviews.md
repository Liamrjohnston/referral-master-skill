# Rewards and reviews

## Rewards: when they help, and when they don't

A reward is not the fix when nobody has been asked in person. A bigger number on an offer no one mentions still gets ignored. Fix the ask first; then a reward can help.

When the owner has or wants a reward:

0. **Fix the ask first.** If nobody has been asked in person, say so before discussing the reward.
1. **Work out what a client is worth** from the owner's figures (see diagnosis.md, "What a client is worth"). Show the working and label it.
2. **Say if it's affordable.** Compare the reward with what a client is worth and, if the owner knows it, what a new client costs them to win now. Hormozi budgets referral rewards against the cost of winning a customer: he would rather pay a happy client than an ad platform.
3. **Recommend the form:**
   - **Thank-you after, chosen for the person** (a dinner, a gift that fits them, a handwritten card). Feels like gratitude, not payment. Best for small service businesses and close relationships.
   - **Two-sided** (something for the referrer and something for the friend). Stops it looking like the referrer is cashing in on a friend. (Hormozi)
   - **Matched to the service** (a free visit, extra hours, an upgrade) so it attracts people who want what the owner sells. (Hormozi)
   - **Status, not cash,** for people who'd feel odd taking money for a friend: first pick of dates, a VIP perk, an invite to a small thank-you event.
   - **Cash to the referrer only, announced to everyone up front:** the weakest option for most service businesses. It makes the ask feel transactional, the friend may resent learning their friend was paid, and clients have told owners they don't want friends to know. (Burg; Jantsch says cash is usually a bad motivator unless the product is cheap)

   The experts disagree on up-front incentives. Hormozi favours a generous, two-sided offer budgeted against the cost of winning a customer, matched to what you sell. Burg and Jantsch prefer no advance incentive and a thank-you after. For a small service business asking person to person, default to thanks after; offer a two-sided, matched perk only if the owner wants an incentive.
4. **Check the rules for the trade.** Some professions restrict or require disclosure of referral fees (for example health professionals, lawyers, financial advisers, real estate agents in many places). If the owner is in a regulated field, tell them to check their regulator's rule before offering money. Don't state a specific rule you aren't sure of.

## Reviews

Reviews help strangers trust the owner. They are not referrals, and they must never be bought.

**Never:**
- A discount, gift, credit, prize draw entry or anything of value in exchange for a review.
- Asking only for 5 stars, or only asking clients you expect to be happy (review gating).
- Writing reviews or testimonials for clients, even with a plan to get sign-off.
- Posting reviews from staff, family or the owner under other names.

Why: it breaks Google's and most platforms' review rules (reviews can be removed and listings penalised), and fake or incentivised reviews can be misleading advertising: Canada's Competition Bureau treats them as deceptive marketing, and the US FTC rule on consumer reviews (2024) bans buying positive reviews. Don't quote penalty amounts.

**Do:**
- Ask every client in a group (for example everyone whose job finished this month) for an honest review, with a direct link, at a good moment.
- Make it easy: one link, one line.
- Reply to every review, good or bad, politely.
- For testimonials, ask the client for a quote in their own words. You can give prompts ("what was it like before, what changed, what would you tell a friend?") and send it back for their OK. Use their real name only with permission.

## Claims the owner wants to make

Check each against the brief. If it isn't true, say so in one line and offer the honest version that does the same job:

| They want | Say instead |
|---|---|
| Scarcity or a deadline that isn't real ("almost full", "ends Friday") | The true version: what is actually open, and an honest reason to book early if there is one. |
| A rounded-up or invented count ("hundreds of clients", "award-winning") | The real number or the real names, or nothing. |
| A result a client reported, stated as the owner's proven result | Attribute it to the client, in their words. |
| Reviews or testimonials written for clients | A request for a real quote, with prompts. |
